span>Room air is exchanged, but particles are first allowed to escape the source and disperse through the hangar.</span><small>Dilution is not source capture</small></button><button class="choice" data-option="2B"><strong>B. Shrouded sander + suitable local extraction with high-efficiency filtration</strong><span>Capture particulate at the point of generation and verify the extraction system, hose, shroud and collector before the task.</span><small>Preferred engineering-control approach</small></button><button class="choice" data-option="2C"><strong>C. Compressed-air tool cleaning between passes</strong><span>Fast, but energetic air can re-suspend settled material and spread it beyond the controlled surface.</span><small>Secondary release risk</small></button></div><div class="mission-actions"><button class="btn primary" id="eval2">Evaluate Stage 2</button></div><div class="feedback" id="fb2"></div></div></div></div></section>

    

    <section id="theory2" class="screen"><div class="page"><div class="page-head"><div><div class="eyebrow">Learning checkpoint 2</div><h2>Capture at source is stronger than dilution</h2><p>The best place to control a particle is before it joins the room air.</p></div></div><div class="theory">
<div class="theory-card"><h3>1. How LEV works</h3><p>Local exhaust ventilation creates airflow that pulls contaminated air into a hood, shroud or extraction port near the generation point. The system then transports the material through ducting or a hose to a collector/filtration unit. Performance depends on the whole chain: capture geometry, distance, airflow, hose condition, filter loading, seals and maintenance.</p><details class="deep"><summary>Deep dive — why position matters</summary><div class="deep-body"><p>NIOSH engineering-control work repeatedly shows that capture efficiency drops as the hood moves away from the source. A beautifully sized extractor can still perform poorly if the shroud is damaged, the hose is kinked or the pickup point is too far from the dust trajectory. The simulator therefore rewards verified source capture rather than a generic “big fan”.</p></div></details></div>
<div class="theory-card"><h3>2. Ventilated sanding tools can materially reduce dust</h3><p>NIOSH has documented major reductions in sanding dust when tools incorporate local exhaust. In one autobody sanding application, ventilated sanders reduced total dust concentrations to roughly one-tenth of unventilated sanding. That figure is not a universal CFRP guarantee, but it illustrates the engineering principle: capture built into the tool can be far more effective than trying to clean the room afterwards.</p></div>
<div class="theory-card"><h3>3. No single airflow number fits every repair</h3><p>Do not teach technicians to memorise one airflow, vacuum rating or hood distance for every composite task. The correct performance criteria depend on the approved tool, material, enclosure, hose length, collector and site procedure. What should be universal is the discipline of verification: adequate suction, intact hoses and seals, correct filter/collector configuration and no visible escape from the shroud.</p></div>
<div class="theory-card"><h3>4. Fine particles matter even when you cannot see a cloud</h3><p>A clean-looking area is not proof that airborne particulate is absent. Machining and sanding can generate fine and respirable fractions. Particle behaviour depends on size, shape and density; smaller particles can remain suspended longer and travel farther with air currents. This is why controls must be based on the process, not on whether the operator sees dust.</p></div>
<div class="theory-card"><h3>5. Carbon-fibre fragments create an additional operational concern</h3><p>Carbon fibres are electrically conductive. Uncontrolled deposition around sensitive electrical/electronic equipment can therefore be undesirable for reliability as well as occupational and environmental reasons. Containment protects the maintenance environment itself, not only the external soil.</p></div>

    <section id="theory3" class="screen"><div class="page"><div class="page-head"><div><div class="eyebrow">Learning checkpoint 3</div><h2>A control failure is a decision point, not a reason to improvise</h2><p>The environmental consequence often depends less on the initial fault than on what happens in the next few minutes.</p></div></div><div class="theory">
<div class="theory-card"><h3>1. First action: stop generation</h3><p>If sanding continues after effective extraction is lost, the task is deliberately creating uncontrolled particulate. The fastest way to reduce the release rate is to stop the abrasive process. That does not mean dismantling the containment or abandoning the repair; it means stabilising the situation before diagnosis.</p></div>
<div class="theory-card"><h3>2. Preserve the containment while you troubleshoot</h3><p>Do not open the enclosure or hangar merely to make the indoor air look cleaner. Keep the boundary intact so that any already-released particulate remains within the smallest manageable area. If site procedures require additional controls, implement them without creating a new pathway.</p></div>
<div class="theory-card"><h3>3. Diagnose the control systematically</h3><p>Common causes can include a blocked or loaded filter, full collector, kinked or disconnected hose, damaged shroud, leak, fan/drive problem or incorrect setup. The exact fault tree is equipment-specific, so follow manufacturer and site instructions. The training principle is to restore <b>verified performance</b>, not simply reset an alarm.</p><details class="deep"><summary>Deep dive — restart criteria</summary><div class="deep-body"><ol><li>The particulate-generating tool is stopped.</li><li>The work-zone boundary remains effective.</li><li>The extraction fault has been identified or the system has been restored through an approved action.</li><li>Capture performance is verified by the required method.</li><li>Any escaped residue has been assessed and controlled.</li><li>The deviation is recorded if required by the local EMS/procedure.</li><li>The task can resume without creating an uncontrolled pathway.</li></ol></div></details></div>
<div class="theory-card"><h3>4. Assess the extent of release before deciding what comes next</h3><p>A few particles inside a sealed bay are not the same event as dust beyond the boundary or in a drain. Check the perimeter, floor, adjacent equipment, footwear/traffic path and relevant drainage points. The response should be proportional to evidence of spread.</p></div>
<div class="theory-card"><h3>5. Mission readiness and environmental control are not enemies</h3><p>The best operational decision often avoids both extremes: neither continue with a failed control nor shut the entire facility indefinitely. A short, disciplined stop can protect the schedule by preventing a much larger cleanup, investigation or rework later. This is how environmental control becomes part of maintenance performance rather than an external constraint.</p></div>
<div class="theory-card wide"><h3>6. EMS response: correction, consequence evaluation and learning</h3><p>Under ISO 14001:2026, the purpose of handling a deviation is to restore control and prevent recurrence. Depending on severity, the organisation may need immediate correction, assessment of environmental consequences, investigation of cause, corrective action and verification that the action was effective. A single event does <b>not</b> automatically cancel certification. What matters is the nature of the nonconformity, systemic weakness, evidence, recurrence and the certification process.</p></div>

    <section id="theory4" class="screen"><div class="page"><div class="page-head"><div><div class="eyebrow">Learning checkpoint 4</div><h2>Housekeeping is part of the engineering control chain</h2><p>The task is not controlled until the residue, tools, boundary and waste route are controlled.</p></div></div><div class="theory">
<div class="theory-card"><h3>1. Settled dust can become airborne again</h3><p>Particulate that has settled is not automatically “safe”. Walking, moving equipment, dry sweeping, brushing and compressed air can re-suspend fine material. NIOSH guidance across dusty industrial tasks repeatedly recommends filtered vacuuming or wet methods instead of dry sweeping/compressed air where appropriate.</p></div>
<div class="theory-card"><h3>2. Vacuum performance depends on filtration and handling</h3><p>A vacuum is useful only if collected fine particulate is not discharged back into the work area. Use equipment and filters approved by the site/task. The operator also needs a safe method for emptying the collector or changing filters because that step can itself release dust.</p><details class="deep"><summary>Deep dive — housekeeping sequence</summary><div class="deep-body"><ol><li>Keep the boundary in place.</li><li>Clean the workpiece and immediate capture zone according to approved repair/cleaning instructions.</li><li>Vacuum or wipe horizontal surfaces from cleaner areas toward the controlled collection point.</li><li>Clean tools, hoses and equipment before they cross the boundary.</li><li>Deal with contaminated PPE/coveralls under the site procedure.</li><li>Seal and label residues/consumables as required.</li><li>Inspect drains, floor protection, corners and traffic routes.</li><li>Only then begin removing temporary containment.</li></ol></div></details></div>
<div class="theory-card"><h3>3. “Wet method” does not mean “wash it down the drain”</h3><p>Damp wiping or controlled wet methods can suppress re-aerosolisation, but they must not create an uncontrolled liquid pathway. A hose directed to a drain may simply transfer polymer/fibre particulate from the floor to wastewater or stormwater. The receiving compartment has changed; the contamination has not disappeared.</p></div>
<div class="theory-card"><h3>4. Waste classification is material- and jurisdiction-specific</h3><p>Do not teach one universal waste code for all cured composite dust. Classification can depend on resin chemistry, coatings, contaminants, repair products, local legislation and site rules. The robust learning point is <b>traceability</b>: know what material was generated, prevent dispersion, collect it in a compatible container and route it through the approved waste process.</p></div>
<div class="theory-card"><h3>5. Remove the containment last</h3><p>If barriers and floor protection are removed before tools, waste and residue are clean, the final minutes of the job can undo the control achieved during sanding. Close-out should move from the contaminated core outward, keeping a clean/dirty logic until the final verification is complete.</p></div>
<div class="theory-card wide"><div class="theory-visual containment"></div><h3 style="margin-top:16px">Why this matters environmentally</h3><p>Soil is a functioning environmental compartment, not simply the ground outside the door. It supports roots, organisms, infiltration and nutrient cycling. The simulator does not claim that every CFRP fragment will immediately damage plants; it teaches the more defensible principle that persistent industrial particulate should not be allowed to escape when a practical prevention route exists.</p></div>
