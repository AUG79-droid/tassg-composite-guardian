    <section id="theory5" class="screen"><div class="page"><div class="page-head"><div><div class="eyebrow">Learning checkpoint 5</div><h2>Verification, nonconformity and certification risk</h2><p>The final lesson is how to close the loop without either minimising the event or overreacting.</p></div></div><div class="theory">
<div class="theory-card"><h3>1. A release check combines physical and management evidence</h3><p>Visual cleanliness is useful but insufficient on its own. A defensible close-out asks whether the boundary remained effective, whether residue and waste are controlled, whether any release pathway shows evidence of contamination, whether the extraction system is restored and whether the deviation has been recorded in the required system.</p></div>
<div class="theory-card"><h3>2. Not every deviation requires the same escalation</h3><p>A short drop in suction that is detected immediately and contained inside the bay is different from a confirmed release to a drain or external soil. The response should be based on consequence, extent, legal/site requirements and recurrence. Risk-based management avoids both complacency and unnecessary shutdown.</p></div>
<div class="theory-card"><h3>3. What a nonconformity actually means</h3><p>A nonconformity is a failure to meet a requirement. In an EMS context, this may involve a procedure not followed, an operational control not maintained, inadequate competence, ineffective corrective action or other system failure. The organisation must correct the issue and address causes and consequences as appropriate.</p></div>
<div class="theory-card"><h3>4. A serious finding does not automatically cancel ISO 14001 certification</h3><p>Certification consequences depend on the certification body’s rules, the nature and extent of the evidence, whether the issue is isolated or systemic, recurrence and the effectiveness/timeliness of corrective action. The training message is therefore: <b>do not hide the deviation</b>. A transparent, well-managed event is stronger evidence of an effective EMS than a missing record.</p></div>
<div class="theory-card wide"><h3>5. The complete control loop</h3><table class="learning-table"><tr><th>Plan</th><th>Do</th><th>Check</th><th>Act</th></tr><tr><td>Identify the aspect, pathways, controls, roles and stop criteria.</td><td>Operate containment, source capture and disciplined work practices.</td><td>Monitor extraction, inspect the boundary, verify housekeeping and record deviations.</td><td>Correct failures, analyse causes when needed, improve the control and verify effectiveness.</td></tr></table><div class="callout"><b>Composite Guardian rule:</b> the job is finished only when the part, the work area, the waste pathway and the management record are all under control.</div></div>

    <section id="knowledge" class="screen"><div class="page"><div class="page-head"><div><div class="eyebrow">Knowledge base · advanced</div><h2>Composite particulate, containment, soil pathways and EMS control</h2><p>This section is intentionally deeper than the mission feedback. It is designed so the game can function as a real learning resource for maintenance personnel who start with little environmental background.</p></div></div>
<div class="kb-grid">
<div class="kb-module"><h3>1. Composite materials: fibre, matrix and repair system</h3><p>Fibre-reinforced polymers combine load-bearing fibres with a polymer matrix. Aerospace structures may use carbon, glass, aramid or other reinforcement systems, with epoxy or other polymer matrices, honeycomb cores, primers, coatings, films and adhesives. During sanding, the debris therefore reflects the <b>whole stack-up</b>, not just the nominal fibre.</p><p>For radomes specifically, electromagnetic transparency is a design requirement, so actual materials are platform-specific and often differ from conventional CFRP structure. This training scenario uses the A400M nose/radome area as operational context only; it does not claim or disclose the real A400M material lay-up or approved repair process.</p></div>
<div class="kb-module"><h3>2. Particle generation and size</h3><p>Abrasive removal fractures the matrix and reinforcement. Particle size depends on abrasive grit, tool speed, contact pressure, material architecture, coating, temperature, wear and capture method. Fine particles can remain airborne longer; larger particles settle faster but can still be tracked out or re-suspended.</p><p>The absence of a visible dust cloud is therefore not evidence of complete control.</p></div>
<div class="kb-module"><h3>3. “Microplastic” is not a synonym for composite dust</h3><p>ECHA describes microplastics as solid plastic particles composed of polymers and additives, generally below 5 mm. Composite sanding debris may include polymer-rich fragments that fit a microplastic concept, but it can also contain carbon or glass fibres, coating fragments and mixed fibre/matrix particles. Good technical communication names the material conservatively unless analytical characterisation supports a more specific claim.</p></div>
<div class="kb-module"><h3>4. Health hazard and environmental hazard are related but not identical</h3><p>Occupational hygiene asks about inhalation, dermal/eye contact and worker exposure. Environmental management asks whether material is released to air, wastewater, stormwater, soil or waste routes. The same control — for example source extraction — can reduce both risks, but the compliance frameworks and receptors differ.</p></div>
<div class="kb-module"><h3>5. LEV fundamentals</h3><p>LEV works by creating a controlled airflow into a hood/shroud, transporting contaminated air and separating particles before cleaned air is discharged or managed according to the system design. Key variables include hood geometry, distance, airflow, pressure loss, hose/duct integrity, filter condition and maintenance. Capture should be as close as practicable to the source.</p><p>General ventilation can dilute a room; it does not guarantee that particles are captured before they deposit elsewhere.</p></div>
<div class="kb-module"><h3>6. Why maintenance and inspection of LEV matter</h3><p>An extraction unit is not a permanent guarantee. Filters load, hoses kink, seals wear, collectors fill and operators reposition tools. A sound process therefore includes pre-use checks, performance indicators or alarms where provided, maintenance and a clear stop criterion if capture degrades.</p></div>
<div class="kb-module"><h3>7. Secondary contamination routes</h3><p>After sanding stops, residue can still migrate via boots, wheels, tools, vacuum handling, waste bags, open doors, dry sweeping, compressed air and wash water. This is why controlled entry/exit and housekeeping belong in the same procedure as the sanding itself.</p></div>
<div class="kb-module"><h3>8. Soil and stormwater as receiving environments</h3><p>Soil supports plants, microorganisms, invertebrates, infiltration and nutrient cycling. Stormwater can transfer small particles beyond the site. The app therefore treats the nearby drain and external strip as pathways that must be protected, without claiming that every composite fragment produces the same ecological toxicity.</p></div>
<div class="kb-module"><h3>9. Housekeeping and waste close-out</h3><p>NIOSH guidance for dusty work repeatedly favours filtered vacuuming and/or wet methods over dry sweeping and compressed air where appropriate, because energetic cleaning can re-suspend dust. Waste handling should then follow the actual material information, contamination state, legal requirements and site procedure. The correct waste code cannot be learned from a generic game.</p></div>
<div class="kb-module"><h3>10. Incident response and nonconformity</h3><p>When a control fails, first stop or reduce the source, preserve containment and assess the extent. Then restore control and document the event according to site rules. If the failure indicates a system weakness, corrective action should address the cause and its effectiveness should be checked. Hiding a deviation prevents organisational learning and can turn a manageable event into a systemic EMS problem.</p></div>
<div class="kb-module"><h3>11. ISO 14001:2026 in plain language</h3><p>ISO 14001:2026 is the current international EMS standard. It provides a framework for identifying environmental aspects, meeting compliance obligations, planning and controlling operations, evaluating performance and continually improving the EMS. It does not prescribe a specific sanding vacuum, filter type or waste container; those details come from legal requirements, engineering assessment, manufacturer instructions and organisational procedures.</p></div>
<div class="kb-module"><h3>12. Mission readiness and proportionality</h3><p>Environmental management in In-Service maintenance has to work with safety, airworthiness, schedule and availability. The strongest decisions are usually those that control the environmental pathway with the least unnecessary operational disruption. A local pause to restore LEV can be better for readiness than allowing contamination that later forces a larger cleanup or investigation.</p></div>
<div class="kb-module wide"><div class="theory-visual lev"></div><h3 style="margin-top:16px">Practical mental model: the five barriers</h3><table class="learning-table"><tr><th>Barrier</th><th>Purpose</th><th>Typical failure question</th></tr><tr><td>1.
