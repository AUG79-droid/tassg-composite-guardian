    <section id="briefing" class="screen">
      <div class="page"><div class="page-head"><div><div class="eyebrow">Mission briefing</div><h2>Radome Repair / Bay 04</h2><p>A composite surface-preparation task has been released in a maintenance hangar. The work generates fine particulate during sanding. A landscaped soil strip and stormwater pathway sit outside the building, so the objective is not simply to protect the technician: it is to keep the particulate inside a defined control chain from generation to final waste collection.</p></div><div class="chips"><span class="chip">Composite repair</span><span class="chip warn">Fine particulate</span><span class="chip warn">Soil & drainage pathway</span></div></div>
      <div class="callout warn"><b>Material accuracy:</b> actual radome construction is platform-specific. Many radar-transparent radomes use glass, quartz or aramid-based systems rather than carbon fibre in RF-critical areas. This serious game uses a generic composite sanding scenario to teach particulate control. The applicable SRM/CMM, approved repair data, SDS and site procedures always govern the real task.</div>
      <div class="theory">
        <div class="theory-card"><h3>What the sanding process actually creates</h3><p>A cured composite is not one substance. It is a combination of reinforcing fibres, a cured polymer matrix, coatings, fillers and repair-system constituents. Abrasive machining can fracture both fibre and matrix, producing particles with different shapes and sizes. Some debris can be coarse and visible; some can be fine enough to remain airborne or be transported by ventilation and foot traffic.</p><p>That is why the simulator uses the term <b>composite particulate</b> rather than treating every fragment as “microplastic”. ECHA describes microplastics as solid plastic particles made of polymers and additives, typically below 5 mm. CFRP sanding debris is heterogeneous: some fragments are polymer-rich, some are carbon-fibre fragments and some contain both.</p></div>
        <div class="theory-card"><h3>Why the environmental pathway matters</h3><p>Once dust crosses the repair boundary, control becomes harder. Particles can settle on the floor, move on footwear and wheels, enter floor drains, be re-aerosolised by sweeping, or leave through open doors. If they reach outdoor soil or stormwater, recovery is much more difficult than preventing the release in the first place.</p><p>The learning objective is therefore a <b>source-to-receptor model</b>: generation → airborne/surface deposition → transfer route → boundary → receiving environment. Every stage of the game closes one link in that chain.</p></div>
        <div class="theory-card wide"><h3>The control strategy used in this game</h3>
          <table class="learning-table"><tr><th>Stage</th><th>Question</th><th>What good control looks like</th></tr>
          <tr><td>1. Isolation</td><td>Where could particles leave?</td><td>Defined bay, controlled access, protected drains/pathways, suitable floor protection and no unnecessary opening to outside.</td></tr>
          <tr><td>2. Source capture</td><td>How do we stop dust becoming room contamination?</td><td>Shrouded or ventilated tooling and local extraction positioned and maintained for the actual task.</td></tr>
          <tr><td>3. Monitoring</td><td>What happens when the control degrades?</td><td>Stop particulate generation, preserve containment, restore the control, assess any release, then restart.</td></tr>
          <tr><td>4. Housekeeping</td><td>How do we remove settled material?</td><td>Methods that minimise resuspension, with residue collected into a controlled waste route.</td></tr>
          <tr><td>5. Release</td><td>What evidence justifies reopening?</td><td>Physical verification, waste close-out, deviation record, corrective action where required and a defensible release decision.</td></tr></table>
        </div>

    <section id="theory1" class="screen"><div class="page"><div class="page-head"><div><div class="eyebrow">Learning checkpoint 1</div><h2>Containment is a pathway-control system</h2><p>A barrier is useful only if it actually interrupts the routes by which particulate can migrate.</p></div></div><div class="theory">
<div class="theory-card"><h3>1. Start with a source–pathway–receptor map</h3><p>The source is the sanding point. The pathways are air movement, floor deposition, footwear, wheels, tools, waste containers, door openings, drains and cleaning water. The receptors are people, adjacent workstations, sensitive equipment, wastewater/stormwater and the external environment. A robust bay is designed around this map rather than around an arbitrary rectangle on the floor.</p><details class="deep"><summary>Deep dive — what should be checked before work?</summary><div class="deep-body"><ul><li>Where is the nearest door or vehicle route and can pressure changes move dust?</li><li>Are there floor drains, pits, cable trenches or cracks that can trap residue?</li><li>Will technicians or tools cross the boundary repeatedly?</li><li>Can the temporary sheeting or enclosure be damaged by hoses, ladders or movement?</li><li>Is there an agreed entry/exit and a method to prevent contaminated footwear or equipment leaving untreated?</li><li>If negative pressure or an enclosed booth is required by the task, has airflow been verified rather than assumed?</li></ul></div></details></div>
<div class="theory-card"><h3>2. PPE and environmental containment solve different problems</h3><p>Respirators, gloves, eye protection and protective clothing can reduce occupational exposure. They do not capture a particle once it has left the tool. Environmental control therefore prioritises elimination/substitution where feasible, engineering controls and controlled work practices. PPE remains essential, but it is not a substitute for source capture or a defined boundary.</p><details class="deep"><summary>Deep dive — why “PPE only” fails the environmental objective</summary><div class="deep-body"><p>A worker can be perfectly protected while the floor, trolley wheels and ventilation system become contaminated. If the task ends with dust outside the work zone, the environmental control has failed even if the technician’s exposure remained acceptable. This distinction is central to the game.</p></div></details></div>
<div class="theory-card"><h3>3. The boundary must be proportional, not theatrical</h3><p>Closing an entire hangar can protect readiness poorly without adding much environmental benefit if the dust source and migration routes remain uncontrolled. Conversely, a very small boundary can fail if air currents, doors or traffic routes bypass it. Good control is risk-based: large enough to manage the actual pathway, but not larger than necessary.</p></div>
<div class="theory-card"><h3>4. Drains and water pathways deserve separate attention</h3><p>Fine particulate that settles on the floor can be mobilised later by wash water or stormwater. Protecting or isolating relevant drains before work prevents a simple housekeeping action from becoming an uncontrolled transfer to wastewater or the outside environment.</p></div>
<div class="theory-card wide"><h3>5. EMS connection: operational control must be demonstrable</h3><p>ISO 14001:2026 is an environmental management system standard, not a prescriptive sanding procedure. The operational lesson is that an organisation should understand its environmental aspects, establish controls for significant risks, maintain those controls, monitor performance and act when deviations occur. A bay that “usually works” but is not checked, maintained or documented is weaker than a proportional control with clear responsibilities and verification.</p><div class="callout"><b>Practical takeaway:</b> Before the tool starts, you should be able to explain where the dust could go, which barriers stop each pathway, who checks them, and what condition would trigger a pause.</div></div>
