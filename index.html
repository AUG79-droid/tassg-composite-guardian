Mission briefing

Radome Repair / Bay 04

A composite surface-preparation task has been released in a maintenance hangar. The work generates fine particulate during sanding. A landscaped soil strip and stormwater pathway sit outside the building, so the objective is not simply to protect the technician: it is to keep the particulate inside a defined control chain from generation to final waste collection.

Composite repairFine particulateSoil & drainage pathway
Material accuracy: actual radome construction is platform-specific. Many radar-transparent radomes use glass, quartz or aramid-based systems rather than carbon fibre in RF-critical areas. This serious game uses a generic composite sanding scenario to teach particulate control. The applicable SRM/CMM, approved repair data, SDS and site procedures always govern the real task.

What the sanding process actually creates

A cured composite is not one substance. It is a combination of reinforcing fibres, a cured polymer matrix, coatings, fillers and repair-system constituents. Abrasive machining can fracture both fibre and matrix, producing particles with different shapes and sizes. Some debris can be coarse and visible; some can be fine enough to remain airborne or be transported by ventilation and foot traffic.

That is why the simulator uses the term composite particulate rather than treating every fragment as “microplastic”. ECHA describes microplastics as solid plastic particles made of polymers and additives, typically below 5 mm. CFRP sanding debris is heterogeneous: some fragments are polymer-rich, some are carbon-fibre fragments and some contain both.

Why the environmental pathway matters

Once dust crosses the repair boundary, control becomes harder. Particles can settle on the floor, move on footwear and wheels, enter floor drains, be re-aerosolised by sweeping, or leave through open doors. If they reach outdoor soil or stormwater, recovery is much more difficult than preventing the release in the first place.

The learning objective is therefore a source-to-receptor model: generation → airborne/surface deposition → transfer route → boundary → receiving environment. Every stage of the game closes one link in that chain.

Learning checkpoint 1

Containment is a pathway-control system

A barrier is useful only if it actually interrupts the routes by which particulate can migrate.

1. Start with a source–pathway–receptor map

The source is the sanding point. The pathways are air movement, floor deposition, footwear, wheels, tools, waste containers, door openings, drains and cleaning water. The receptors are people, adjacent workstations, sensitive equipment, wastewater/stormwater and the external environment. A robust bay is designed around this map rather than around an arbitrary rectangle on the floor.

Deep dive — what should be checked before work?
  • Where is the nearest door or vehicle route and can pressure changes move dust?
  • Are there floor drains, pits, cable trenches or cracks that can trap residue?
  • Will technicians or tools cross the boundary repeatedly?
  • Can the temporary sheeting or enclosure be damaged by hoses, ladders or movement?
  • Is there an agreed entry/exit and a method to prevent contaminated footwear or equipment leaving untreated?
  • If negative pressure or an enclosed booth is required by the task, has airflow been verified rather than assumed?

2. PPE and environmental containment solve different problems

Respirators, gloves, eye protection and protective clothing can reduce occupational exposure. They do not capture a particle once it has left the tool. Environmental control therefore prioritises elimination/substitution where feasible, engineering controls and controlled work practices. PPE remains essential, but it is not a substitute for source capture or a defined boundary.

Deep dive — why “PPE only” fails the environmental objective

A worker can be perfectly protected while the floor, trolley wheels and ventilation system become contaminated. If the task ends with dust outside the work zone, the environmental control has failed even if the technician’s exposure remained acceptable. This distinction is central to the game.

3. The boundary must be proportional, not theatrical

Closing an entire hangar can protect readiness poorly without adding much environmental benefit if the dust source and migration routes remain uncontrolled. Conversely, a very small boundary can fail if air currents, doors or traffic routes bypass it. Good control is risk-based: large enough to manage the actual pathway, but not larger than necessary.

4. Drains and water pathways deserve separate attention

Fine particulate that settles on the floor can be mobilised later by wash water or stormwater. Protecting or isolating relevant drains before work prevents a simple housekeeping action from becoming an uncontrolled transfer to wastewater or the outside environment.

5. EMS connection: operational control must be demonstrable

ISO 14001:2026 is an environmental management system standard, not a prescriptive sanding procedure. The operational lesson is that an organisation should understand its environmental aspects, establish controls for significant risks, maintain those controls, monitor performance and act when deviations occur. A bay that “usually works” but is not checked, maintained or documented is weaker than a proportional control with clear responsibilities and verification.

Practical takeaway: Before the tool starts, you should be able to explain where the dust could go, which barriers stop each pathway, who checks them, and what condition would trigger a pause.

Learning checkpoint 2

Capture at source is stronger than dilution

The best place to control a particle is before it joins the room air.

1. How LEV works

Local exhaust ventilation creates airflow that pulls contaminated air into a hood, shroud or extraction port near the generation point. The system then transports the material through ducting or a hose to a collector/filtration unit. Performance depends on the whole chain: capture geometry, distance, airflow, hose condition, filter loading, seals and maintenance.

Deep dive — why position matters

NIOSH engineering-control work repeatedly shows that capture efficiency drops as the hood moves away from the source. A beautifully sized extractor can still perform poorly if the shroud is damaged, the hose is kinked or the pickup point is too far from the dust trajectory. The simulator therefore rewards verified source capture rather than a generic “big fan”.

2. Ventilated sanding tools can materially reduce dust

NIOSH has documented major reductions in sanding dust when tools incorporate local exhaust. In one autobody sanding application, ventilated sanders reduced total dust concentrations to roughly one-tenth of unventilated sanding. That figure is not a universal CFRP guarantee, but it illustrates the engineering principle: capture built into the tool can be far more effective than trying to clean the room afterwards.

3. No single airflow number fits every repair

Do not teach technicians to memorise one airflow, vacuum rating or hood distance for every composite task. The correct performance criteria depend on the approved tool, material, enclosure, hose length, collector and site procedure. What should be universal is the discipline of verification: adequate suction, intact hoses and seals, correct filter/collector configuration and no visible escape from the shroud.

4. Fine particles matter even when you cannot see a cloud

A clean-looking area is not proof that airborne particulate is absent. Machining and sanding can generate fine and respirable fractions. Particle behaviour depends on size, shape and density; smaller particles can remain suspended longer and travel farther with air currents. This is why controls must be based on the process, not on whether the operator sees dust.

5. Carbon-fibre fragments create an additional operational concern

Carbon fibres are electrically conductive. Uncontrolled deposition around sensitive electrical/electronic equipment can therefore be undesirable for reliability as well as occupational and environmental reasons. Containment protects the maintenance environment itself, not only the external soil.

Learning checkpoint 3

A control failure is a decision point, not a reason to improvise

The environmental consequence often depends less on the initial fault than on what happens in the next few minutes.

1. First action: stop generation

If sanding continues after effective extraction is lost, the task is deliberately creating uncontrolled particulate. The fastest way to reduce the release rate is to stop the abrasive process. That does not mean dismantling the containment or abandoning the repair; it means stabilising the situation before diagnosis.

2. Preserve the containment while you troubleshoot

Do not open the enclosure or hangar merely to make the indoor air look cleaner. Keep the boundary intact so that any already-released particulate remains within the smallest manageable area. If site procedures require additional controls, implement them without creating a new pathway.

3. Diagnose the control systematically

Common causes can include a blocked or loaded filter, full collector, kinked or disconnected hose, damaged shroud, leak, fan/drive problem or incorrect setup. The exact fault tree is equipment-specific, so follow manufacturer and site instructions. The training principle is to restore verified performance, not simply reset an alarm.

Deep dive — restart criteria
  1. The particulate-generating tool is stopped.
  2. The work-zone boundary remains effective.
  3. The extraction fault has been identified or the system has been restored through an approved action.
  4. Capture performance is verified by the required method.
  5. Any escaped residue has been assessed and controlled.
  6. The deviation is recorded if required by the local EMS/procedure.
  7. The task can resume without creating an uncontrolled pathway.

4. Assess the extent of release before deciding what comes next

A few particles inside a sealed bay are not the same event as dust beyond the boundary or in a drain. Check the perimeter, floor, adjacent equipment, footwear/traffic path and relevant drainage points. The response should be proportional to evidence of spread.

5. Mission readiness and environmental control are not enemies

The best operational decision often avoids both extremes: neither continue with a failed control nor shut the entire facility indefinitely. A short, disciplined stop can protect the schedule by preventing a much larger cleanup, investigation or rework later. This is how environmental control becomes part of maintenance performance rather than an external constraint.

6. EMS response: correction, consequence evaluation and learning

Under ISO 14001:2026, the purpose of handling a deviation is to restore control and prevent recurrence. Depending on severity, the organisation may need immediate correction, assessment of environmental consequences, investigation of cause, corrective action and verification that the action was effective. A single event does not automatically cancel certification. What matters is the nature of the nonconformity, systemic weakness, evidence, recurrence and the certification process.

Learning checkpoint 4

Housekeeping is part of the engineering control chain

The task is not controlled until the residue, tools, boundary and waste route are controlled.

1. Settled dust can become airborne again

Particulate that has settled is not automatically “safe”. Walking, moving equipment, dry sweeping, brushing and compressed air can re-suspend fine material. NIOSH guidance across dusty industrial tasks repeatedly recommends filtered vacuuming or wet methods instead of dry sweeping/compressed air where appropriate.

2. Vacuum performance depends on filtration and handling

A vacuum is useful only if collected fine particulate is not discharged back into the work area. Use equipment and filters approved by the site/task. The operator also needs a safe method for emptying the collector or changing filters because that step can itself release dust.

Deep dive — housekeeping sequence
  1. Keep the boundary in place.
  2. Clean the workpiece and immediate capture zone according to approved repair/cleaning instructions.
  3. Vacuum or wipe horizontal surfaces from cleaner areas toward the controlled collection point.
  4. Clean tools, hoses and equipment before they cross the boundary.
  5. Deal with contaminated PPE/coveralls under the site procedure.
  6. Seal and label residues/consumables as required.
  7. Inspect drains, floor protection, corners and traffic routes.
  8. Only then begin removing temporary containment.

3. “Wet method” does not mean “wash it down the drain”

Damp wiping or controlled wet methods can suppress re-aerosolisation, but they must not create an uncontrolled liquid pathway. A hose directed to a drain may simply transfer polymer/fibre particulate from the floor to wastewater or stormwater. The receiving compartment has changed; the contamination has not disappeared.

4. Waste classification is material- and jurisdiction-specific

Do not teach one universal waste code for all cured composite dust. Classification can depend on resin chemistry, coatings, contaminants, repair products, local legislation and site rules. The robust learning point is traceability: know what material was generated, prevent dispersion, collect it in a compatible container and route it through the approved waste process.

5. Remove the containment last

If barriers and floor protection are removed before tools, waste and residue are clean, the final minutes of the job can undo the control achieved during sanding. Close-out should move from the contaminated core outward, keeping a clean/dirty logic until the final verification is complete.

Why this matters environmentally

Soil is a functioning environmental compartment, not simply the ground outside the door. It supports roots, organisms, infiltration and nutrient cycling. The simulator does not claim that every CFRP fragment will immediately damage plants; it teaches the more defensible principle that persistent industrial particulate should not be allowed to escape when a practical prevention route exists.

Learning checkpoint 5

Verification, nonconformity and certification risk

The final lesson is how to close the loop without either minimising the event or overreacting.

1. A release check combines physical and management evidence

Visual cleanliness is useful but insufficient on its own. A defensible close-out asks whether the boundary remained effective, whether residue and waste are controlled, whether any release pathway shows evidence of contamination, whether the extraction system is restored and whether the deviation has been recorded in the required system.

2. Not every deviation requires the same escalation

A short drop in suction that is detected immediately and contained inside the bay is different from a confirmed release to a drain or external soil. The response should be based on consequence, extent, legal/site requirements and recurrence. Risk-based management avoids both complacency and unnecessary shutdown.

3. What a nonconformity actually means

A nonconformity is a failure to meet a requirement. In an EMS context, this may involve a procedure not followed, an operational control not maintained, inadequate competence, ineffective corrective action or other system failure. The organisation must correct the issue and address causes and consequences as appropriate.

4. A serious finding does not automatically cancel ISO 14001 certification

Certification consequences depend on the certification body’s rules, the nature and extent of the evidence, whether the issue is isolated or systemic, recurrence and the effectiveness/timeliness of corrective action. The training message is therefore: do not hide the deviation. A transparent, well-managed event is stronger evidence of an effective EMS than a missing record.

Knowledge base · advanced

Composite particulate, containment, soil pathways and EMS control

This section is intentionally deeper than the mission feedback. It is designed so the game can function as a real learning resource for maintenance personnel who start with little environmental background.

1. Composite materials: fibre, matrix and repair system

Fibre-reinforced polymers combine load-bearing fibres with a polymer matrix. Aerospace structures may use carbon, glass, aramid or other reinforcement systems, with epoxy or other polymer matrices, honeycomb cores, primers, coatings, films and adhesives. During sanding, the debris therefore reflects the whole stack-up, not just the nominal fibre.

For radomes specifically, electromagnetic transparency is a design requirement, so actual materials are platform-specific and often differ from conventional CFRP structure. This training scenario uses the A400M nose/radome area as operational context only; it does not claim or disclose the real A400M material lay-up or approved repair process.

2. Particle generation and size

Abrasive removal fractures the matrix and reinforcement. Particle size depends on abrasive grit, tool speed, contact pressure, material architecture, coating, temperature, wear and capture method. Fine particles can remain airborne longer; larger particles settle faster but can still be tracked out or re-suspended.

The absence of a visible dust cloud is therefore not evidence of complete control.

3. “Microplastic” is not a synonym for composite dust

ECHA describes microplastics as solid plastic particles composed of polymers and additives, generally below 5 mm. Composite sanding debris may include polymer-rich fragments that fit a microplastic concept, but it can also contain carbon or glass fibres, coating fragments and mixed fibre/matrix particles. Good technical communication names the material conservatively unless analytical characterisation supports a more specific claim.

4. Health hazard and environmental hazard are related but not identical

Occupational hygiene asks about inhalation, dermal/eye contact and worker exposure. Environmental management asks whether material is released to air, wastewater, stormwater, soil or waste routes. The same control — for example source extraction — can reduce both risks, but the compliance frameworks and receptors differ.

5. LEV fundamentals

LEV works by creating a controlled airflow into a hood/shroud, transporting contaminated air and separating particles before cleaned air is discharged or managed according to the system design. Key variables include hood geometry, distance, airflow, pressure loss, hose/duct integrity, filter condition and maintenance. Capture should be as close as practicable to the source.

General ventilation can dilute a room; it does not guarantee that particles are captured before they deposit elsewhere.

6. Why maintenance and inspection of LEV matter

An extraction unit is not a permanent guarantee. Filters load, hoses kink, seals wear, collectors fill and operators reposition tools. A sound process therefore includes pre-use checks, performance indicators or alarms where provided, maintenance and a clear stop criterion if capture degrades.

7. Secondary contamination routes

After sanding stops, residue can still migrate via boots, wheels, tools, vacuum handling, waste bags, open doors, dry sweeping, compressed air and wash water. This is why controlled entry/exit and housekeeping belong in the same procedure as the sanding itself.

8. Soil and stormwater as receiving environments

Soil supports plants, microorganisms, invertebrates, infiltration and nutrient cycling. Stormwater can transfer small particles beyond the site. The app therefore treats the nearby drain and external strip as pathways that must be protected, without claiming that every composite fragment produces the same ecological toxicity.

9. Housekeeping and waste close-out

NIOSH guidance for dusty work repeatedly favours filtered vacuuming and/or wet methods over dry sweeping and compressed air where appropriate, because energetic cleaning can re-suspend dust. Waste handling should then follow the actual material information, contamination state, legal requirements and site procedure. The correct waste code cannot be learned from a generic game.

10. Incident response and nonconformity

When a control fails, first stop or reduce the source, preserve containment and assess the extent. Then restore control and document the event according to site rules. If the failure indicates a system weakness, corrective action should address the cause and its effectiveness should be checked. Hiding a deviation prevents organisational learning and can turn a manageable event into a systemic EMS problem.

11. ISO 14001:2026 in plain language

ISO 14001:2026 is the current international EMS standard. It provides a framework for identifying environmental aspects, meeting compliance obligations, planning and controlling operations, evaluating performance and continually improving the EMS. It does not prescribe a specific sanding vacuum, filter type or waste container; those details come from legal requirements, engineering assessment, manufacturer instructions and organisational procedures.

12. Mission readiness and proportionality

Environmental management in In-Service maintenance has to work with safety, airworthiness, schedule and availability. The strongest decisions are usually those that control the environmental pathway with the least unnecessary operational disruption. A local pause to restore LEV can be better for readiness than allowing contamination that later forces a larger cleanup or investigation.
